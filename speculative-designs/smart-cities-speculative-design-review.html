Foundations 01

From "the what" to "the how"

Social infrastructure is usually defined by its places. The more useful definition defines it by whether connection actually happens there.

The common definition

What is social infrastructure?

The physical places, organizations, and spaces — schools, hospitals, parks, libraries — that foster social connection, community cohesion, and quality of life.

In other words, the network of shared social experiences within a given location (i.e. the "what").
Reframing · NYU perspective

Not a collection — a system

Social infrastructure is not just a collection of shared places, but an actively designed system whose quality determines whether meaningful social connection and community resilience actually emerge.

In other words, the network of shared spaces that make social connection more likely to occur (i.e. the "how").
Challenges and constraints
Constraint 01

Coordination & funding

Fragmented governance and limited resources make cohesive and sustained design difficult to achieve.

Constraint 02

Land use tradeoffs

Competing demands for space restrict how effectively social infrastructure can be placed and integrated.

Constraint 03

Legacy constraints

Existing urban layouts and infrastructure limit how easily social systems can be reorganized or improved.

Constraint 04

Equity considerations

Design decisions can unintentionally improve access for some groups while disadvantaging others.

Constraint 05 · the new one

Data governance

Smart-city systems add new questions about privacy, surveillance, consent, and accountability.

Smart-city systems design (UCD PERSPECTIVE)

Smart-city systems design uses technologies such as sensors, automation, digital twins, connected services, and other data platforms to manage or improve city life.

The lens for every case that follows

For this perspective, we will investigate access, trust, and responsiveness to the lived experience — all as part of the system they operate within.

Success Case

Barcelona Superblocks

Barcelona, Spain

Using smart-city tools and street redesign to reclaim urban space for pedestrians, local activity, and neighborhood interaction. It showcases a social infrastructure designed around lived experience, allowing streets to become places for social life, not just movement.

WHO

Neighborhood residents, pedestrians, children, older adults, cyclists, disabled people, local businesses, and anyone using streets as public space rather than just traffic space.

WHAT

It sought to reduce through-traffic and reclaim street space for walking, greenery, recreation, and neighborhood interaction.

HOW

The model emphasizes public participation, dialogue, and shared responsibility. Residents are involved in identifying problems, discussing proposals, and shaping implementation.

WHY IT MATTERS

Streets are the most abundant public space a city already owns — the question is only what they're allowed to be used for.

CONCLUSION

This aligns with contextual inquiry and participatory design because it starts from how people actually use streets, not only from traffic models.

Risks

The main risks are gentrification, displacement, business disruption, accessibility conflicts, emergency-access issues, and backlash from people who feel the redesign was imposed on them.

Opportunities

They could measure impacts on different groups, protect against displacement, improve accessibility, support multilingual communication, test emergency access, and create feedback channels for residents to request adjustments.

Cautionary Case

Songdo IBD

Songdo, South Korea

Built with advanced sensors, automation, and connected infrastructure, but criticized for struggling to generate the organic social and cultural life that makes cities feel lived-in. It shows the danger of technology-first urbanism: a city can be efficient, green, and connected while still feeling socially thin or exclusionary.

WHO

The intended users were international businesses, knowledge workers, professionals, expatriates, universities, investors, city managers, and residents imagined as part of a high-tech global city.

WHAT

It sought to solve many problems at once, largely centered around sustainability — automated waste systems, centralized building controls, green buildings, water recycling, walkability, and public transit.

HOW

It was mostly shaped by government, developers, and technology firms. Residents appear to have had a weaker role than institutional and investor stakeholders.

THE TELL

The users were imagined before they were studied — a research bias baked in before the first sensor was installed.

CONCLUSION

Institutional stakeholders appear to have been prioritized over research involving everyday residents. Ultimately, they introduced bias into the research phase too early, in favor of "better" users.

Risks

The main risks were designing for an idealized user rather than real, diverse residents. Additionally, the city may have prioritized control, efficiency, and image over flexibility, social life, affordability, and everyday needs.

Opportunities

They could have begun with ethnographic research into daily life — mixed-income housing, integration with surrounding communities, participatory governance, adaptable public spaces, resident feedback loops, and attention to informal social life.

Edge Case

Smart Nation / TraceTogether

Singapore

A national initiative integrating digital identity, public services, and urban data systems to make daily life more connected, efficient, and governable. It shows both sides of public digital infrastructure: adoption often depends on trust and state capacity — but trust can be damaged when data-use boundaries are unclear.

WHO

Residents, visitors, public-health officials, contact tracers, businesses checking people into venues, older adults, children, and people without smartphones.

WHAT

Initially created for COVID-19 contact tracing — identifying close contacts faster and more accurately than manual recall, via Bluetooth signaling. It was later repurposed for criminal investigation without user acknowledgment.

HOW

The system incorporated feedback through social media, interviews, engagement programs, and accessibility measures for people who had difficulty using smartphones.

THE BREACH

Police were legally able to access the data for criminal investigations in secret — the mission crept, and the public found out afterward.

CONCLUSION

This is a service design problem because the experience depended on devices, businesses, public spaces, institutions, and trust. While cases like this can be common, even this singular instance damaged trust.

Risks

Privacy concerns, mission creep, mandatory participation, uneven digital literacy, and loss of trust when data use expanded beyond what users expected. The biggest issue was police being legally able to access the data for criminal investigations in secret.

Opportunities

It should have included clearer consent, narrower data-use limits from launch, independent oversight, public auditability, plain-language privacy explanations, deletion or opt-out options where possible, and equivalent non-digital access.

Rejected Case

Sidewalk Toronto / Quayside

Toronto, Canada

A data-driven neighborhood proposed around sensors, adaptive infrastructure, and urban data systems — which collapsed after public concerns about privacy, trust, and data governance. It shows that even advanced urban technology can fail if people do not trust who controls it, who benefits, and what happens to their data.

WHO

Future residents, pedestrians, cyclists, workers, visitors, city agencies, developers, technologists, and local businesses.

WHAT

It aimed to create a technology-enabled waterfront district using sustainability features, mobility improvements, modular buildings, digital services, and data-driven urban management.

HOW

There was significant public engagement, including conversations with more than 21,000 local residents during development of the initial plan. While they began with a privacy commissioner to include privacy-by-design, they ultimately parted ways in favor of privacy-by-suggestion.

THE DISTINCTION

21,000 conversations still weren't co-design. Volume of engagement is not the same as power over the outcome.

CONCLUSION

Engagement is not the same as participatory co-design, and privacy-by-design is often the only way to gain user trust. People need real influence over problem framing, technical commitments, and governance.

Risks

Privacy, surveillance, unclear data governance, privatization of public infrastructure, weak accountability, and public distrust. It also affected people nearby who may never have explicitly opted into data collection.

Opportunities

Privacy and governance should have been designed from the beginning. It needed clear data limits, independent oversight, community power over surveillance technologies, and genuine co-design before major technical commitments were made.

Emerging Case

Helsinki 3D

Helsinki, Finland

Using open data, digital twins, and participatory planning to make urban services more transparent, adaptive, and citizen-centered. It is a strong planning interface — but its value depends on whether it is usable by residents, not only by professionals.

WHO · DIRECT

Planners, architects, engineers, researchers, developers, analysts, and public agencies.

WHO · BROADER

Residents, community groups, businesses, and educators trying to understand how it affects them.

WHAT

It sought to create digital city models that help people test, visualize, and plan changes in the built environment.

HOW

The public has been invited to virtually explore city models, which can make planning more visible and easier to understand.

CONCLUSION

This shifts planning from abstract documents to interactive representations — but only if the interface is usable by non-experts.

Risks

Expert bias, technical complexity, unequal access, over-reliance on simulation, and performative participation.

Opportunities

They should include plain-language scenarios, accessible interfaces, mobile-friendly tools, multilingual explanations, neighborhood-level impact views, and direct ways for residents to comment on proposed changes.

Design Trends 04

Design trends across cases

Read side by side, the five cases fall into the same habits — and point to the same missing ones.

Design patterns used
These cases relied on:
  • Top-down planning
  • Efficiency-first systems
  • Data-driven management
  • Idealized user assumptions
  • Weak consent models
→
Design patterns needed
Useful additions to them are:
  • Privacy by default
  • Meaningful alternatives
  • Visible accountability
  • Accessibility (early)
  • Feedback loops (post)

Discussion 06

Discussion questions

Four questions the five cases leave open.

01
When does smart-city infrastructure stop being a public service and start becoming surveillance infrastructure?
02
Who should have authority over urban data — governments, private firms, residents, or independent public bodies?
03
How can designers include people who are affected by smart-city systems but who do not directly use them?
04
Should cities prioritize efficient digital systems if those systems weaken trust, informality, or community control?
Engagement is not co-design. People need real influence over problem framing, not just the plan.
