How Might We the question
HMW

How might we help both a dementia patient and their caregiver navigate a recognition mismatch without distress?

The problem

A person may live in an earlier moment of their life — and being corrected usually deepens distress on both sides.

The system

"Memory Space" splits into two synchronized views — one shown to the patient, one kept privately for the caregiver.

The principle

The technology disappears. What's left isn't an interface — it's a calmer, more natural moment of human connection.

Context 01

When the present becomes another time

Dementia frequently produces recognition mismatch. A person may experience the present as an earlier point in their life, relating to the time and people around them as they remember them rather than as they are in the moment.

Meanwhile, the caregiver is left to absorb the strain of not being recognized. Direct correction in these moments usually worsens the resulting distress on both sides.

The design tension

Two people share the exact same moment — and need opposite things from it.

The process
Step 01 · Stakeholder research
Surface the real clinical constraints
Begin with stakeholder research to understand the constraints that actually shape these moments — before proposing anything.
Step 02 · Low-fidelity storyboards
Sketch the emotional experience first
Move from low-fidelity storyboards into the feel of the interaction, where changes cost a pencil rather than a headset.
Step 03 · Wizard-of-Oz prototypes
Simulate the overlays before hardware
Build Wizard-of-Oz prototypes that simulate the AR overlays, testing the experience before committing to any device.
Step 04 · Observational testing
Learn in supervised care settings
Run observational testing with clinicians, iterating on how the system infers the patient's perceived time and delivers caregiver cues.
Throughout

Treat clinicians and caregivers as co-designers whose lived experience drives each iteration — brought in early and often, so the system is shaped by the people who will actually use it.

The System 02

Two views of the same moment

"Memory Space" is a two-sided AR/MR dementia-care system that bridges recognition gaps by adapting what patients see, while privately coaching caregivers through emotionally difficult moments.

one moment
The guiding principle

Mixed reality works best when the technology disappears and the interaction feels intuitive — every unnecessary technical detail stripped away.

Memory Space applies that principle to dementia care, editing the perceived environment so that what remains is not an interface — but a calmer, more natural moment of human connection.

Rationale 03

Meet people where they are

The approach meets patients within their reality instead of forcing correction, aligning with validation-based dementia care that reduces agitation. Splitting the system into a patient view and a caregiver view respects that these two users have opposite needs in the very same moment.

Keeping the mediation invisible — so the technology fades and only the human interaction remains — keeps cognitive load low for a population that cannot manage complex interfaces. Starting with storyboards and simulated overlays lets me test the emotional experience cheaply before investing in AR hardware.

Centering clinician input early exposes failure modes — such as unpredictable shifts in perceived time and patients ignoring screens — and pushes the design toward caregiver support rather than patient-facing screens.

Forcing correction

Insisting on the present reality reminds the patient of what they've lost — and reliably escalates agitation on both sides.

Risks & Challenges 04

A system that gently bends reality carries real risks. Each one shapes the design rather than sitting beside it.

Challenge 01

Unpredictable reality shifts

Patients change who and when they think they are.

ResponseThe system updates live, and lets caregivers adjust by hand.
Challenge 02

Patient comprehension

Some patients won't engage with a screen at all.

ResponseThe caregiver side leads; patient AR stays optional.
Challenge 03

Information capture

It's hard to auto-detect where the patient's mind is.

ResponseCaregivers supply that input simply.
Challenge 04

Ethical risk of deception

Bending reality could feel manipulative.

ResponseThe design follows validation-therapy norms and loops in families.
Challenge 05

Hardware burden

Headsets are a lot for frail users to carry.

ResponseFavor lighter devices and a gradual roll-out.
The throughline

Every response leans the same way: keep the caregiver in control, and keep the patient's dignity at the center.

Alignment with Design Statement 05

How the proposal stays honest

Bridging disciplines

The two-sided design only works when clinical insight, engineering, and design come together.

Early & continuous involvement

Contextual inquiry — and treating clinicians and caregivers as co-designers — grounds the work in real behavior rather than assumptions.

Empirical, iterative refinement

Storyboards lead to Wizard-of-Oz prototypes and then observational testing, learning from each round before building hardware.

Systems as a web of impacts

Second-order effects — like the ethics of deception — alongside technical and financial feasibility keep the design usable and sustainable.

Two views, one moment — and the technology, at its best, disappears.
