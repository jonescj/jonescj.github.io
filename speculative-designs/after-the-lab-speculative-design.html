How might we design wearable augmentation systems that extend the body's awareness and capability in real-world use, not just in the lab?

Context 01

The device works. The adoption doesn't.

The Hero Arm is a 3D-printed, myoelectric bionic arm for below-elbow amputees, available to adults and children as young as eight. It reads residual muscle signals and translates them into grip movements — but roughly one in four users abandons their device, most citing the absence of sensory feedback and poor real-world fit.

Rationale 03

Why this device, for this question

Prosthetic success depends on real-world fit, not just technical capability. A device can pass every lab benchmark and still fail if it is uncomfortable, socially awkward, too heavy, or poorly matched to a user's actual routines.

The Hero Arm is especially useful for design research because it combines function, pediatric access, and cost accessibility into one product — making trade-off decisions visible in ways that purely functional devices do not.

Risks & Challenges 04

Five ways an evaluation like this can mislead — or run into limits that design alone cannot fix.

Challenge 01

Aesthetic bias over utility

The superhero framing attracts media but may obscure how the device performs in less glamorous daily tasks.

Challenge 02

Population flattening

Users differ by age, cause of limb difference, occupation, and cultural attitude toward visible disability.

Challenge 03

Sensory ceilings

No commercially available myoelectric system currently includes meaningful touch or position-sensing feedback.

Challenge 04

Insurance complexity

Coverage varies by state, insurer, and documentation — making equitable access a structural problem that better design alone cannot solve.

Challenge 05

Lab-to-life transfer

Strong performance in grip tasks during clinical trials does not predict whether someone will still be wearing the device six months later.

The throughline

Some of these are research risks. Others — insurance, sensory ceilings — are limits on what any redesign can reach. Naming which is which is part of the evaluation.

Alignment with Design Statement 05

Where the Hero Arm meets my method — and where it doesn't

Early & continuous user involvement

The 2025 Hero PRO and Hero RGD were shaped by feedback from roughly 1,000 existing users over four years — reflecting the principle that decisions should be validated by the people living with them.

Contextual inquiry

Studying how the arm performs within the web of daily systems, not in isolation, aligns directly with how I ground my own work in real environments and behaviors.

Minimizing exclusion

The Hero Arm is cheaper than most competitors.

The gapBut it still navigates insurance systems, geographic availability, and age restrictions — all of which exclude people in ways technical improvement alone cannot resolve.
The verdict

The Hero Arm gets more right than most. The remaining failures are the ones that live outside the lab — and outside the product.

A device doesn't succeed when it works. It succeeds when it's still being worn.
