The Approach 02

A Tiered Accountability Architecture

Here, we propose a Tiered Accountability Architecture (TAA), a harm-zoned permission system built into an agent's operating layer. Entering a higher-risk zone means pre-consenting to a higher level of oversight. This is an attempt to evaluate accountability less as reaction but an ingrained condition of access.

What boundary contact triggers
Mechanism 01

Rotating cryptographic barriers

Contact with a boundary raises a barrier that rotates, so access can't be quietly reused or replayed.

Mechanism 02

Real-time owner notifications

The agent's owner is told as it happens — not after a report is compiled.

Mechanism 03

Tiered escalation

Contact escalates to the appropriate stakeholders for the zone the agent has entered.

Mechanism 04

Visual audit trail

Every path the agent has taken is rendered as a trail anyone can read and act on.

  • Core · operating layerWhere the agent runs. Accountability is a property of this layer, not a wrapper around it.
  • Zone 1 · low riskEntry is visible to users in real time, so monitoring is never a surprise.
  • Zone 2 · elevatedBoundary contact triggers rotating barriers and real-time owner notification.
  • Zone 3 · high riskAccess requires pre-consent to escalation, routed to the appropriate stakeholders.

Rationale 03

From outside monitoring to structural accountability

Current solutions monitor agents from the outside. None of them are designed for the two people who feel the consequences: the agent owner and the affected user.

Today · monitored from outside
  • Sandboxes contain damage after the fact.
  • Observability tools log decisions.
  • Alerts notify developers.

None of these are designed for the agent owner or the affected user.

→
Proposed · accountability by design

The proposed solution reframes the problem so that accountability becomes a structural property of how the agent runs — not a report generated after something goes wrong.

Risks & Challenges 04

Building accountability into the operating layer introduces its own failure modes. Each is paired with a mitigation.

Challenge 01

Consent fatigue

MitigationAlert on patterns of contact, not every individual touch.
Challenge 02

Defining harm

MitigationMitigate through participatory zone definition with diverse stakeholders.
Challenge 03

False positive deletion

MitigationA mandatory appeal window should precede any permanent deletion.
Challenge 04

Chilling effect

MitigationZone 1 entry should be visible to users in real time so monitoring is never a surprise.
Challenge 05

Key management UX

MitigationAbstract cryptography entirely into plain-language controls non-engineers can use.
Principle

Every mitigation keeps the same goal in view: oversight that a non-expert can actually understand, trust, and act on.

Alignment with Design Statement 05

How the proposal holds to its principles

Bridging disciplines

The visual audit trail translates cryptographic governance into something a business owner or advocate can act on without engineering knowledge.

Adaptability over rigidity

Harm zones and escalation chains are reconfigurable as contexts change, not locked in at deployment.

Trust through reliability

Consistent, predictable boundary enforcement builds long-term trust with non-expert users.

Minimizing exclusion

Plain-language controls ensure non-technical owners can govern systems acting on their behalf.

Contextual inquiry

Harm zones are co-developed with users through contextual inquiry grounded in real environments and behaviors.

Iterative prototyping

The permission UX is validated through Wizard of Oz prototyping before any technical build begins.

Accountability as an ingrained condition of access — not a report written after the damage.
