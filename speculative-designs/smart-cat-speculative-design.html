Comfort by Contact

A responsive companion object that communicates warmth, presence, and reassurance through touch, scent, vibration, and expression — responding only when you reach for it.

TL;DR at a glance
Opportunity

Many wellness tools depend on screens, language, notifications, or deliberate navigation — least useful when someone is stressed, lonely, cognitively tired, or just wants a quiet form of reassurance.

Concept

A companion object activated through familiar gestures. Petting releases a chosen scent and changes the cat's expression; hugging or squeezing produces warmth, low-frequency vibration, and a more open posture.

Key decision

Explicit, user-controlled interaction over passive mood detection. The product never decides you need comfort — it responds only to intentional touch, and every output can be configured or disabled.

Scope note

Not a medical device. The study did not claim the object could treat anxiety, depression, or loneliness — it examined how multimodal feedback makes a comfort object feel responsive and understandable.

Overview 01

Responsive without becoming another screen

Smart Cat investigated touch, scent, warmth, vibration, and visual expression as parts of one coordinated interaction system. Instead of menus or dashboards, the object responds to gestures already associated with animals and comfort objects. Two related concepts were developed in parallel.

Coco
Emission & expression

Responds to a gentle stroke across the head or cheek. A recognized petting gesture triggers a brief scent response while the eyes widen to show the interaction was accepted.

Emphasis: environmental reassurance and sensory association.

Rumble
Heating & vibration

Responds when the user squeezes or hugs its belly. The cat becomes warm, produces a low-frequency vibration, and opens its arms to communicate availability and presence.

Emphasis: physical warmth, contact, and companionship.

The Problem 02

Comfort objects don't acknowledge you. Screens acknowledge too much.

A blanket, plush toy, candle, or heating pad may feel soothing, but it doesn't respond to the person. Digital wellness products respond — but often demand screens, text, configuration, and sustained attention, which feels procedural at exactly the moment someone wants something simpler.

The design challenge — a product that could
  • Respond through physical rather than screen-based interaction
  • Use familiar gestures instead of abstract controls
  • Confirm that an input had been recognized
  • Offer more than one sensory channel
  • Avoid activating accidentally
  • Preserve user control over when comfort features were used
How might we…

…design a responsive comfort object that acknowledges familiar gestures through coordinated sensory feedback without deciding what the user feels or needs?

The tension that shaped everything

Passive sensing

A companion that senses mood could feel intelligent and animal-like — but it could also misread the user, activate unwanted outputs, or make intrusive assumptions about their emotional state.

Active touch

A product requiring intentional touch places more responsibility on the user — but makes the interaction predictable and consensual. This is the path the study committed to.

Research & Interaction Framing 03

Translating social gestures into recognizable inputs

The study focused less on broad market research and more on a harder question: not only what the product should do, but how it would distinguish meaningful interaction from accidental contact. Two gestures were chosen for the social meanings they already carry.

Petting
Communicates attention, calm, familiarity, and social bonding. For Coco, a stroke across the head became the input for a light scent response — mapping petting to scent-marking and familiarity.
Hug / squeeze
Communicates a desire for warmth, reassurance, or presence. For Rumble, sustained pressure around the belly activated heat and vibration — the vibration suggesting a purr or heartbeat, the warmth reinforcing contact.
Why multimodal — and why the eyes move first

Each concept used more than one output, so sensory redundancy could help users understand the system without relying on a single cue. The visual response was functional: it confirmed the gesture was recognized before a slower output like scent or heat became noticeable.

Synthesis 04

Five requirements — one of them a principle

The concept work produced five primary design requirements. The last one became the study's most important outcome.

Requirement 01

Use intentional gestures

Respond to sustained strokes or squeezes rather than taps, bumps, or brief contact — so a user can handle or reposition the object without triggering it.

Requirement 02

Make recognition visible

A delayed scent or temperature change leaves users uncertain. Visual or physical movement should begin first, confirming the input was accepted.

Requirement 03

Match output to input

Longer or firmer interaction can produce a stronger, more sustained response — but only within predefined limits. No unrestricted heat, scent, or vibration.

Requirement 04

Configuration & shutdown

Scent, heat, and vibration each on or off; output intensity, duration, and preferred combinations. Physical switches keep it controllable without a companion app.

Requirement 05 · the principle

Avoid emotional inference

The product should not assume that petting means anxiety or that hugging means loneliness. The gesture communicates a request for a chosen output — not a diagnosis of the user's state. This distinction became one of the most important outcomes of the study.

Testing & Prototypes 06

Where the concept met the material

The prototype used an existing plush cat with simulated outputs rather than integrated sensors and actuators. That gap exposed several differences between a conceptual interaction and a physically credible one.

Coco · the scent wouldn't stop
Observation

Fragrance sprayed onto the plush was retained by the fabric long after the input ended — impossible to represent an output that stops shortly after the gesture.

Adjustment

Fragrance was sprayed near the participant instead of onto the object, so the scent appeared closer to the moment of interaction and dissipated faster.

Production implication

Internal cartridge, controlled micro-diffuser, directional vent, replaceable/washable components, strict output limits, a scent-free mode — and no absorption by the outer material.

Rumble · the warmth lagged
Observation

Warming the whole plush took too long to heat and cool. The delay weakened the perceived link between squeezing and warmth, and made repeated testing hard.

Adjustment

A preheated hand warmer inside the cat's clothing simulated a localized element — more immediate, though not true real-time activation and deactivation.

Production implication

Localized heating pad, internal temperature sensors, automatic shutoff, a conservative ceiling, even distribution, thermal insulation around electronics, and a safe-transfer surface.

Material limitations

The plush exterior supported the emotional concept but created engineering problems. Thick fur could interfere with capacitive touch sensing, pressure recognition, heat transfer, ventilation, cleaning, and scent dissipation.

A production object might use thinner fabric, embedded pressure zones, or smooth panels in selected interaction areas — balancing the softness of a comfort object with the reliability of a sensor-equipped device.

Final Design Direction 07

A deliberate companion, not a predictive one

The final direction combined the strongest parts of both concepts into a configurable Smart Cat platform. We considered ambient mood sensing — a cat that notices changes in its owner — but retained explicit physical activation, for four reasons.

Consent

The user chooses when an output begins.

Predictability

A recognized gesture produces a known result.

Privacy

No voice, facial, behavioral, or biometric data needed to infer mood.

Personalization

The user decides which outputs comfort them and which stay disabled.

Proposed interaction system
Pet the head
Scent · facial expression · posture change
Squeeze / hug the body
Warmth · vibration · open arms · breathing-like motion
Release
Outputs stop or return gradually to their default state
Physical panel
Concealed but accessible: power, scent, heat, vibration, intensity, response duration
Why two zones

Separating head and belly created a simple physical vocabulary — a stroke requests a lighter environmental response, a squeeze a stronger bodily one. That reduced the need for menus while keeping outputs intentional and learnable.

Reflection 08

Multimodal isn't more outputs — it's their timing

The project showed that multimodal interaction is not simply the addition of several outputs. The timing and relationship among those outputs determine whether the product feels responsive. Coco's widened eyes weren't decorative — they compensated for the delay before scent release. Rumble's arm movement did the same, confirming the squeeze was recognized before warmth became noticeable.

The prototype also revealed that material behavior can determine whether an interaction concept is viable at all. Plush fabric retained fragrance, reduced heat transfer, and complicated touch sensing — not minor engineering details, but forces that directly changed how the interaction needed to be designed.

The strongest decision was retaining active user control. Passive mood detection might have made Smart Cat appear more intelligent, but it would have required the product to interpret personal behavior and decide when intervention was appropriate — creating privacy, trust, and consent problems disproportionate to its purpose.

Would need further work before development
  • Testing with users who have sensory sensitivities
  • Lower, independently verified heat limits
  • Allergy-safe and scent-free configurations
  • Washable or removable external materials
  • Quiet motors and localized heating
  • Battery and charging safety
  • Child and pet safety
  • Accessible controls for limited grip strength
  • Clear indication of active outputs
  • Long-term testing for habituation and annoyance

I would also test Coco and Rumble as separate products before combining them. Scent and heat create very different technical, safety, and maintenance requirements. A modular platform is appealing conceptually, but a narrower first product would be more feasible.

The broader lesson

A responsive companion does not need to infer emotion to feel attentive. It can communicate presence by recognizing a deliberate gesture, responding clearly, and giving the user control over what happens next.

Presence, not prediction.
